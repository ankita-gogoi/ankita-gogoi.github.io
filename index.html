10-K Text & Sentiment Analysis

Goal

Annual reports (10-Ks) are long, dense documents. I wanted to measure their language at scale: how positive, negative or legally cautious they sound, how hard they are to read, and how much they talk about the future.

Data

100 10-K filings, downloaded and processed in Python (Jupyter notebooks).

Approach

  • Built a corpus-wide term-frequency dictionary and scored each filing against financial sentiment lexicons (positive, negative, litigious), normalized by document length.
  • Segmented text with NLTK and isolated forward-looking statements using modal-verb detection.
  • Measured readability (Gunning Fog, complex-word share) and computed every metric in parallel for the full text and the forward-looking subset.

Results

  • About 9.5% of sentences in a typical 10-K are forward-looking.
  • Forward-looking sentences carry far more sentiment: positive words appear at 3x the rate of the full text (0.33% vs. 0.10%) and negative words at 2.6x (1.29% vs. 0.50%). Negative words still outnumber positive ones by roughly 4 to 1.
  • Forward-looking sentences are also much harder to read, with an average Fog score nearly double that of the full text.

MD&A Extraction

Goal

Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis (MD&A), is where management explains the company's results in its own words. I wanted to compare this section's language with the language of the 10-K as a whole.

Approach

  • Built a rule-based parser to locate and extract Item 7 across inconsistently formatted filings.
  • Applied the full 10-K pipeline to the MD&A and saved the combined dataset as a pickle for reuse.

Results

  • Successfully extracted the MD&A from 89 filings, averaging about 16,000 words each.
  • The MD&A is far more expressive than the rest of the 10-K: positive words appear at about 6x the full-text rate (0.59% vs. 0.10%) and negative words at about 3.4x (1.71% vs. 0.50%), while litigious language is lower.
  • It is also more forward-looking: 21.7% of MD&A sentences are future-oriented, compared with 9.5% across the full 10-K. It is easier to read as well, with a lower Fog score.

Earnings Call Scraper

Goal

Build a dataset of earnings calls that separates what executives say in their prepared remarks from how they answer analysts in the Q&A.

Data

Earnings call transcripts from 10 pages of The Motley Fool's transcript listings, 20 calls per page.

Approach

  • Automated transcript collection across 10 listing pages with a Python scraper.
  • Segmented each call into prepared remarks and Q&A, then parsed it into speaker turns.
  • Structured the output as two linked tables: one at the call level and one at the speaker-turn level.

Results

  • Collected and parsed 200 earnings calls into 8,603 speaker turns, about 43 turns per call.
  • Produced a clean, labeled dataset (prepared remarks vs. Q&A) that became the training data for the Naive Bayes classifier in Project 4.

Prepared vs. Q&A Classifier

Goal

Do executives talk differently when they're reading a script than when they're answering questions live? I built a model to find out.

Approach

  • Trained a Naive Bayes classifier on executives' prepared remarks versus their live answers to analysts.
  • Deployed the model in an interactive web page that returns a label and class probabilities for any sentence.

Results

  • Reached 96.6% accuracy on 732 held-out test examples using the real class priors (18% prepared, 82% Q&A), compared with 95.4% under equal priors.
  • Most errors were Q&A answers labeled as prepared remarks. Nearly all were executives' closing statements, which tend to be rehearsed, so the model's mistake is understandable.
  • On the web page, I tested the model on earnings call statements outside the training and test data. Accuracy improved as inputs got longer.
